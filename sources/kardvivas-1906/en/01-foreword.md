## Foreword

Fencing is one of the noblest and at the same time most useful sports. It develops and strengthens body and soul alike. It increases personal courage and develops self-confidence. It secures a calm, brave and manly bearing even in difficult circumstances. It is therefore absolutely necessary that every educated man — the soldier especially — acquire this skill to the greatest possible degree.

It goes without saying that fencing cannot be learned from a book. Good results can be achieved only at the price of exercises carried out with tireless diligence. The elementary things must be learned and practised *slowly and exactly*, and only then — gradually, in step with the pupils' progress — should one pass on to the actual bout. Systematic instruction is therefore needed here.

That someone is a good fencer does not mean that they can also teach at once. My former pupils — now troop officers — confirmed me in this truth of experience: when they were entrusted with fencing instruction with the troops, they turned to me for guidance in countless cases.

With the help of my book, masters as well as troop officers can teach purposefully. To promote this, in Chapter III I use the *"master–pupil"* method.

In institutions, teaching is a very difficult task because of the large number of pupils. The master must therefore arouse the pupils' interest. If the manner of teaching is light, interest stays awake; so we ease things for beginners by having the exercises prescribed in §§5–12 practised at first *"in the basic stance"*. The *"foot and body movements"* are not neglected on this account either, because the young men in military education — by the time they come to instruction in sabre fencing — already know them anyway.

If we had pupils who have not yet learned to fence, then with them too the *"foot and body movements"* need not be practised separately. Instead, already in the *first lesson* we put a sabre in their hands, make them acquainted with the sabre grip, teach them one or two sabre swings, and show them the *"tierce position"* and, starting from it, the fencing stance; after that, foot and body movements are to be practised continuously, alternating with the handling of the sabre.

[p. VIII]

I wrote my book on the basis of 15 years' experience. In compiling it I was helped by Arlow's work entitled *A kardvívás* ("Sabre Fencing"), from which I drew valuable data. Captain László Székely of the Honvéd was so kind as to assist in preparing my work for the press; for the trouble taken I express my grateful thanks here.

Throughout my work so far I have become thoroughly convinced of the expediency of the system set out in my work, and I hope that the other masters — working in a similar capacity — will approve of it as well.

(The instructor should draw up for themselves, according to the number of weekly hours, a schedule for the whole teaching period. In this there is no need to follow the order of the book to the letter; rather, at their own discretion, they should lay more weight on whatever causes their pupils greater difficulty, and also repeat it more often.)

Leszák Károly,
fencing master.

> **Translator's notes.**
> - "Troop officers" renders *csapattisztek*: officers serving with the troops (as opposed to the academy).
> - "Handling of the sabre" renders *kardforgatás* (literally "turning/wielding the sabre"), here meaning practice with the sabre as against practice of the feet and body.
> - The foreword's teaching scheme matters for later use of this book: §§5–12 are taught first **in the basic stance** (feet fixed, no footwork), and only then in the fencing stance with foot and body movements (Chapter II). Beginners get the sabre in hand in the first lesson: grip → one or two swings → tierce position → fencing stance.
> - I have not identified "Arlow" or the work *A kardvívás* mentioned here; the name is given as printed.
